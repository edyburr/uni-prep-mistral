Curs 05 — Signal conditioning, ADC & DAC

Conspect: introducere în condiționarea semnalului · ADC · DAC · tehnici de procesare · studii de caz.

Introduction to signal conditioning

  • Signal characteristics: amplitude, frequency, period, phase, noise and interference (artifacts), polarity.
  • Sensor/transducer types: biosensors (medical sensors) — temperature, glucose, motion, pressure, pH, gas sensors, etc.
  • The signal conditioning process: amplification of the signal, filtering of the signal, analog-to-digital conversion (ADC), digital-to-analog conversion (DAC).
  • Signal processing: isolation and securing of the signal, transmission of the signal, monitoring and diagnosis.

Analog-to-digital conversion (ADC)

  • Role: allows transforming analog data into a digital format for later processing. Many physical quantities we measure are analog; ADCs act as intermediaries, taking these analog values and transforming them into a digital format that computers and digital systems work with efficiently.
  • Precision and accuracy: the quality of ADCs used in medical devices can directly affect the precision of medical diagnoses and patient safety — incorrectly digitized data can lead to erroneous conclusions with potentially serious consequences.
  • Sampling rate: dictates how many samples per second are taken from the analog signal; a higher sampling rate offers a more precise representation of the analog signal, especially for fast-changing signals.
  • Resolution: the level of detail in the digital representation — the number of bits used to represent an analog signal (8-bit, 12-bit, 16-bit). Higher resolution = smaller variations can be detected and represented.
  • Once digitized, data can be easily stored, analyzed and transmitted — essential for long-term patient monitoring, sharing with healthcare professionals, or the electronic health record.

ADC types

  • Parallel (flash): among the fastest and most precise; take all bits of the analog signal simultaneously and convert them in parallel, giving high sampling rates. Used in real-time processing applications such as communication or laboratory equipment.
  • Successive approximation (serial): convert one bit at a time, going through all bits successively; slower than parallel but can offer high precision; useful in high-resolution data measurement and control systems.
  • Dual-slope: known for precision and handling high-noise signals; perform two successive approximations, one fast and one fine; used in medical applications such as medical imaging.
  • Integrating: compare the analog signal with a ramp; the time needed for the ramp to reach the signal value determines the value; used in oscilloscopes and test equipment.
  • Interleaved: combine several ADC channels to increase the sampling rate; useful when several signals must be acquired at the same time, as in medical data acquisition systems.
  • Choosing the right ADC type is essential for adequate performance and precision in signal conditioning.

ADC parameters

  • Resolution: number of bits (8, 12, 16); higher resolution records smaller variations.
  • Sampling rate: samples/s; essential in capturing fast-changing signals.
  • Precision: ability to make exact and consistent measurements; can be influenced by systemic errors such as offset, which must be minimized.
  • ENOB (effective number of bits): quantization errors and intrinsic ADC noise affect precision; a 12-bit ADC in a system may effectively have only 9.5 bits.
  • Dynamic range: the interval of values the ADC can measure between its minimum and maximum.
  • Latency: time between taking an analog sample and the digital result being available; small latency is crucial in real-time applications.
  • Power consumption: important in mobile/battery devices — low-power ADCs preferred.
  • Noise: unwanted variations in the digital signal due to interference or quantization errors.
  • Channel cross-talk: interference between multiple ADC channels, which can contaminate data.
  • Linearity: how well the ADC represents a proportional increase of the analog signal; crucial to avoid data distortion.

Practical considerations for choosing an ADC

  • High-resolution ADCs for applications where precise measurements are crucial — ECG, medical imaging; lower resolution may suffice for less critical applications.
  • Applications monitoring fast-changing physiological parameters (e.g. blood pressure) may need high sampling rates.
  • Ensure the ADC can handle the input voltage range; in some medical applications input signals vary widely.
  • Low noise levels are essential in medical equipment where precision is crucial.
  • Compatibility with the microcontroller/processor and data formats must be considered.
  • Battery-powered devices: choose ADCs with minimal power requirements.

Real-world case studies

  • ECG monitoring device: a 16-bit ADC with a sampling rate of at least 500 Hz is chosen to ensure precise capture of ECG waveforms; low noise performance helps distinguish subtle variations, suitable for diagnostic purposes.
  • Pulse oximeter: measures oxygen saturation and pulse rate; speed and low power are key — a 12-bit ADC with a high sampling rate (1000 Hz) captures fast pulse changes; design focuses on low power consumption for extended portable battery life.

Digital-to-analog conversion (DAC)

  • Role: transform digital signals back into analog form — important wherever precise control or generation of analog signals is needed in medical devices.
  • Controlling signal outputs: e.g. in an infusion pump, a DAC precisely controls the drug flow rate — the digital control signals are converted into corresponding analog voltages that regulate the pump motor speed.
  • Waveform generation: in ECG devices, DACs produce the analog signals that create the ECG waveform; in ultrasound and MRI scanners, DACs generate the waveforms (frequency and amplitude) that drive the transducers / gradient coils.
  • Audio and speech processing: in hearing aids, DACs convert digital audio signals into analog sound waves delivered to the user's ear.
  • Closed-loop control: in automated anesthesia systems and insulin pumps, DACs contribute to closed-loop control, adjusting the drug administration rate based on real-time data (e.g. a continuous glucose monitor feeds glucose values to a DAC that adjusts the insulin rate to keep glycemia in a target range).
  • Calibration and testing: DACs generate known analog signals for testing and calibrating sensors and measurement equipment.
  • Modern cardiac pacemakers use DACs to generate precise stimulation impulses — the digital control input determines the rate and intensity of the impulses.

DAC basics, types and parameters

  • A DAC converts discrete digital values into a continuous, smooth analog signal (voltage or current).
  • Resolution: number of discrete steps in its output range, expressed in bits (8, 12, 16) — more steps = more precise analog signal.
  • Output range: interval of voltages or currents it can generate (min to max) — important for compatibility with the connected circuit.
  • Types: binary weighted, R-2R ladder, voltage-output, current-output, mixed-output, voltage-scaling (output directly proportional to the digital input regardless of load), current-scaling (current directly proportional to the digital input, independent of output voltage).
  • Most DACs receive digital data through a digital interface such as SPI or I2C, allowing precise control.
  • Precision and errors: errors such as offset and scale (gain) offset can affect precision and must be considered.
