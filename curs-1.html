Curs 01 — Biosignals, electrodes, protection & buffer

Conspect: potențialul de acțiune · electrozi · etajul de protecție · etajul tampon.

Introduction — what is medical electronics?

  • Part of biomedical engineering; applies electronics in the medical field, for diagnosis or treatment.
  • In diagnosis, electronic apparatus offers information about the patient, with which the state of health or illness can be evaluated. Sometimes the apparatus can even evaluate the information and directly provide the diagnosis.
  • Information can be electric (electrodes capturing the bioelectric signal — ECG, EEG, EMG) or non-electric (transducers converting a non-electric signal into an electric one — temperature, pressure, weight, force).
  • In treatment, electronic apparatus acts through a transfer of energy of various types onto the patient, or contributes to other processes for therapeutic purposes.
  • Focus of medical electronics: patient safety, measurement precision, strict regulatory compliance, and electromagnetic compatibility — devices must work correctly in the presence of other electronic equipment without producing or suffering interference.

The action potential

  • Generated by the depolarization and repolarization of the cell. When several cells are excited simultaneously, action potentials sum up (e.g. depolarization of the heart muscle cells generates potential variations detectable at the limb extremities).
  • Such signals are called biosignals; the ones most often measured are ECG, EMG, EEG. Parameters usually measured: amplitude and frequency.
  • Resting potential: in the resting state the cell has a negative membrane potential (approx. -70 mV) due to the unequal distribution of Na⁺ and K⁺ ions.
  • Polarization: a stimulus opens ion channels, positive ions enter and slightly raise the cell potential.
  • Depolarization: if the potential reaches a threshold, voltage-dependent sodium channels open, Na⁺ enters rapidly, reversing the membrane polarity — the interior becomes positive, approx. +30 mV, for 1 ms.
  • Repolarization: sodium channels close, potassium channels open; K⁺ exits the cell, restoring the negative internal polarity in approx. 1–2 ms. During this time the cell cannot generate a new action potential.
  • Resting potential restored: the sodium-potassium pump redistributes the ions (expels Na⁺ and takes in K⁺), bringing the cell back to its initial resting state.
  • In nerve and muscle cells, repolarization is so fast that the action potential appears as a spike with a total duration of only 1 ms; the whole wave lasts approx. 3–5 ms. The action potential of the heart muscle usually lasts 150–300 ms.

Biosignal characteristics

  • Heart: amplitudes 100 µV … 4 mV, spectrum 0.1 Hz … 100 Hz → electrocardiography (ECG/EKG).
  • Brain: amplitudes 10 µV … 100 µV, spectrum 0.1 Hz … 100 Hz → electroencephalography (EEG).
  • Neuromuscular system: amplitudes 100 µV … 4 mV, spectrum 5 Hz … 2 kHz → electromyography (EMG).
  • ECG table values: peak-to-peak 2 mV, spectrum 0.05–100 Hz; EEG scalp electrode 50 µV, 0.5–100 Hz; EMG surface electrode 1 mV, 10–5000 Hz.
  • Electromedical apparatus in contact with the human body are classified in two categories: functional — receiver of biomedical information, or transfers a certain form of energy through the connections to the body.

The human body as an electrical resistance

  • 1,000,000 Ω is considered the maximum electrical resistance of the human body under ideal coupling conditions to a voltage source. The body almost never has this idealized resistance.
  • Regardless of resistance, once current passes through the body, we talk about the intensity that makes it dangerous. Voltage only has the role of triggering (pressure factor, forcing insulation breakdown) the current's entry into the body. Once the current has passed the skin's insulating layer, only the intensity matters — that is what kills or merely tickles.

Effects of current through the body (50 Hz, 1 s, between the two arms)

  • 1 mA — threshold of sensation.
  • 5 mA — accepted maximum harmless current intensity.
  • 10–20 mA — threshold of permanent contraction.
  • 50 mA — pain, possible fainting, mechanical injury; cardiac and respiratory activity continue.
  • 100–300 mA — ventricular fibrillation; respiration continues.
  • 6 A — temporary respiratory paralysis; persistent myocardial contraction followed by normal rhythm; burns if current density is high.
  • Maximum safe current, 0 Hz … 1 kHz, applied to the cardiac muscle: only 10 µA.
  • Example: through a wet body with resistance 3 kΩ flows 6 mA, the voltage on the casing reaches 18 V, and the short-circuit current to ground can reach 90 A, through a ground resistance Rg = 0.2 Ω.
  • Effects are maximal around the 50 Hz mains frequency. For frequencies above 200 kHz, the current no longer has a stimulator effect.

Surface electrodes

  • To measure a bioelectric signal, electrodes placed on the body surface or inside the body are used.
  • The human body is a quasi-electrolyte (ionic conduction); the electrode is metallic (electronic conduction). At the interface, phenomena transform ionic conduction into electronic and vice versa.
  • Dry electrode: metal applied directly on the skin. Wet electrode: metal applied through an electrolyte — wet electrodes are preferred.
  • Active electrode: has a pre-amplification module immediately after the conductive material between skin and electrode.

Equivalent circuit

  • Biological channel: the areas of the organism that the biosignal (Et) traverses to reach the body surface; generally good conductivity through blood-irrigated tissues (Rt, Ct).
  • Skin: reduced and especially variable conductivity (Rp), influenced by tissue fluid amount, sweat gland activity, blood irrigation, cornification of the superficial layer; not a pure resistance — it also has a capacitive component.
  • Skin-electrode interface — the most restrictive crossing zone, equivalent to a voltage source, the half-cell potential Ei, in series with a parallel grouping Ci, Ri, in parallel with a leakage resistance Rfi. The half-cell potential is a disturbing signal source and must be reduced as much as possible.
  • Electrode: resistive behavior (Re). Differential input of the amplifier: resistive behavior (Rd).

Requirements imposed on electrodes

  • Not influence the biological process; optimal uptake of the useful signal and rejection of artifacts from the biological channel and external electrical disturbances.
  • Stability over time of characteristics under mechanical, physical and chemical disturbances.
  • Robust, easy, non-intrusive for the patient; simple and fast handling; low cost.
  • General recommendations: use electrodes with as low a half-cell potential as possible; choose body areas as immobile and undeformed as possible; make skin contact zones as large as possible; clean the skin before applying electrodes; use a pad soaked in conductive liquid, gel or electrode paste as intermediary.
  • Sensors are the interface between the electronic instrument and the biological system; they can be invasive or non-invasive. At surface sensors, conductive gel is used to reduce skin-electrode impedance. Single-use surface electrodes already contain conductive gel. In a medical instrument, sensors are usually the components that deteriorate fastest, leading to inaccurate, "noisy" measurements.

Protection stage (circuitul de protecție)

  • I/O and power pins connecting a device to the outside world are a major source of failures due to EMI (electromagnetic interference) and ESD (electrostatic discharge).
  • IEC/EN 60601-1-2 defines the type of interference medical devices must withstand. Interference must not cause: failure in diagnosis or treatment; errors (artifacts, distortions) inducing wrong actions; false alarms; cessation of intended operations or activation of unintended ones; modifications of parameters or internal memory followed by false alarms; modifications of operating modes; failures in components or PCB boards such as short-circuits or broken PCB traces.

Conformity levels (test / reference / level)

  • ESD — EN 61000-4-2: ±6 kV contact, coupling ±8 kV air.
  • Radiated RF immunity — EN 61000-4-3: 3 V/m, from 80 MHz to 2.5 GHz.
  • Electrical fast transients — EN 61000-4-4: ±2 kV on AC input and I/O cables.
  • Surge immunity — EN 61000-4-5: ±1 kV differential mode, ±2 kV common mode.
  • Conducted RF immunity — EN 61000-4-6: 3 Veff/m, from 150 kHz to 80 MHz.
  • Power-frequency magnetic field immunity — EN 61000-4-8: 3 A/m at 50/60 Hz.

Transient voltage suppressors (TVS)

  • Role: clamp transient overvoltage to an acceptable level. Below the threshold, an ideal TVS is an open circuit; above the threshold it immediately becomes a short circuit with unlimited current flow.
  • Thresholds: silicon diode 0.7 V, germanium 0.3 V, Zener 1.8–200 V, Schottky 5–600 V, transistor 0.8 V, thyristor (SCR, Triac) 25–700 V, MOV (metal-oxide varistor) 5 V–1.8 kV, GDT (gas discharge tube) 60 V–5 kV.

Buffer stage (etajul tampon)

  • It is the first stage of the instrumentation, so noise and interference must be considered carefully.
  • The noise introduced by this stage must be much smaller than the measured input signal.
  • The input impedance must be much larger than the source impedance.
  • The maximum admissible overvoltage depends on the protection circuit characteristics and the limiting device.
  • The buffer must support the input dynamic range plus all permitted interference without modifying the source signal beyond the prescribed limits, and adds limited noise regarding the measurement.
  • Final key requirement: the input signal must not be modified by the linear or nonlinear performance of the buffer stage.
  • The buffer must not introduce attenuation at specific frequencies where the source signal is significant. If the signal shape is significant, as in ECG, the buffer must have a linear phase property: all frequency components in the passband must have the same time delay.
  • Ideal buffer behavior (op-amp): voltage gain, infinite input impedance, zero output impedance, flat magnitude response with zero phase shift.
