Curs 04 — Band-stop, notch & filter topologies

Conspect: filtrul oprește bandă · filtrul notch · alte tipologii de filtre.

Active band-stop filter

  • All input frequencies below 200 Hz are transmitted unattenuated by the low-pass filter; all frequencies above 800 Hz are transmitted unattenuated by the high-pass filter. Frequencies between 200 Hz and 800 Hz are rejected by each filter, forming a notch in the output response.
  • Implementation: a single low-pass and a single high-pass filter, isolated from each other by a voltage follower (Av = 1), then re-summed using a third op-amp connected as a summer.
  • If the feedback resistor and the two input resistors of the summer all have the same value (e.g. 10 kΩ), the inverting summer provides an algebraically correct sum of the two input signals with zero voltage gain.

Notch filter

  • Simple band-stop filters reject a wide band; a notch filter is a very selective, high-Q design used to reject a single or very narrow band of frequencies — e.g. mains noise induced in a circuit with inductive loads (motors, ballast lighting), or harmonics.
  • The most common notch design is the twin-T network: two RC branches in a T shape, using three resistors and three capacitors, with R and C elements opposite in the T part of the design, creating a deeper notch.
  • Active notch: the Q value is determined by the ratio of R3 and R4; to make Q fully adjustable, replace them with a potentiometer; to obtain the maximum notch depth at a given frequency, R3 and R4 can be eliminated and the R–2C junction connected directly to the output.

Filter order, decades and octaves

  • The order of a filter depends on the number of reactive components (capacitors or inductors) in its design. The width of the transition band depends on the filter order: for a simple first-order filter it is 20 dB/decade or 6 dB/octave.
  • Higher-order filters (3rd, 4th, 5th…) are usually formed by cascading single first and second order filters.
  • A decade is a tenfold change on the frequency scale (×10 or ÷10): 2→20 Hz is one decade; 50→5000 Hz is two decades.
  • An octave is a doubling or halving (×2 or ÷2): 10→20 Hz is one octave; 2→16 Hz is three octaves.
  • Logarithmic scales are widely used in the frequency domain for amplifiers and filters.

Filter approximation topologies

  • An ideal filter would offer maximum gain and flatness in the passband, minimum attenuation in the stopband, and a very abrupt transition band. Mathematical "approximation functions" are used in analog filter design: Elliptical (Cauer), Butterworth, Chebyshev, Bessel, and others.
  • Bessel (Friedrich Bessel): the softest response of all; even without a sharp cutoff, it offers superior phase delay. Needs the most stages (more components), with excellent characteristics and low sensitivity to component tolerance. Ideal for applications needing minimal phase change; usable only where there is adequate space between passband and stopband.
  • Butterworth (Stephen Butterworth): maximally flat passband; better selectivity than many other topologies, at the cost of greater phase delay and weaker phase linearity. Not very sensitive to tolerances of discrete components. Most often used filter.
  • Chebyshev (Pafnuty Chebyshev): known for its rippled response — in the passband (Type 1) or in the stopband (Type 2). Ripple amplitude is directly proportional to the cutoff slope: steeper cutoff = larger ripple. Performance between Elliptic and Butterworth; easy to realize with few components and very good selectivity with one of the steepest cutoffs.
  • Elliptic / Cauer (Wilhelm Cauer): ripple in both passband and stopband (passband ripple similar to Chebyshev) with much better selectivity; disadvantage is a more complex filtering network needing more components. Best used where selectivity is a key design factor; ripple in both bands can be adjusted separately.
  • Best filter? Depends on many weighed elements: frequency response, selectivity, complexity (number of stages), phase delay. Most often: Butterworth.
